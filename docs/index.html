Abstract

Can observable search-performance and content signals be combined into a transparent ranking that helps a content team prioritize pages for review? I built a rule-based content opportunity score using content age, CTR, impressions, and average position from the FlyRank internship warehouse. The resulting queue ranks 71,667 eligible pages and attaches reason codes, review confidence, and a human-review action. A supervised model experiment was also tested, but its target was derived from historical CTR, making clicks and impressions target-related inputs; the validation audit therefore invalidates the 1.000 Precision@20 result as a publishable model claim. The final artifact keeps the transparent baseline as the safer decision-support mechanism and turns its ranked output into an auditable action playbook.

1 · Problem statement

Content teams need a practical way to decide which pages deserve attention first. A binary “refresh / do not refresh” rule loses ranking information; a transparent score can instead create a queue that humans can inspect, challenge, and act on.

Research question: Can a transparent scoring rule prioritize content pages for refresh or optimization review using observable search-performance and content signals?

The output is a ranked queue. It does not claim that a high score causes a refresh to improve traffic, clicks, or rankings.

3 · Methodology

Baseline score = 0.30 × age + 0.30 × low CTR + 0.30 × impressions + 0.10 × near-page-one

Each signal is normalized. The baseline is deliberately simple and interpretable: the score tells a reviewer why a page surfaced, while reason codes translate signal patterns into an actionable review path.

Modeling experiment — and why it was rejected

Week 5 tested Logistic Regression and Random Forest using a binary target defined from the top 20% of historical CTR. CTR itself was removed from the feature set, but clicks and impressions remained as predictors even though they are components of CTR. Both models reported Precision@20 = 1.000.

Validation finding: the Week-6 audit identified this target relationship as a leakage risk. Therefore the 1.000 model result is retained only as a diagnostic finding, not as evidence of deployable model quality. The transparent baseline remains the final action engine.

5 · Limitations & honest framing

  • The queue is a prioritization and review aid, not a causal estimator.
  • A high score does not prove that refreshing content will improve traffic, clicks, or rankings.
  • Content-level aggregation can hide query-level differences.
  • The reporting window and available warehouse fields constrain inference.
  • Missing or incomplete metadata can reduce recommendation confidence.
  • Historical metadata does not guarantee a perfect point-in-time snapshot for every decision.
  • The supervised experiment used a proxy target tied to historical CTR, so its high score should not be treated as generalization evidence.

6 · Ranked recommendations

  1. Refresh content: prioritize visible pages showing age/decay signals and weak engagement, then verify intent and current quality.
  2. Improve the snippet: when visibility is high but CTR is weak, inspect title, description, SERP features, and intent before editing content.
  3. Support page-one / striking-distance pages: consider authority, internal linking, and ranking context before rewriting.
  4. Monitor low-volume pages: avoid over-interpreting weak evidence.
  5. Hold for review: when available signals do not support a confident action.
Operational rule: Score → Rank → Explain → Human Review → Decide → Measure.

No action should be executed solely because a page received a high queue rank. The Week-7 playbook explicitly treats the queue as non-production decision support.

7 · Reproducibility

The analysis is organized in the repository's work/notebooks/ path, with the capstone notebook mirroring this paper. The final action queue is exported as work/outputs/content_action_queue.csv; the action summary is work/outputs/content_action_summary.csv; and the queue chart is work/figures/action_queue_by_action.png.

For the supervised experiment, the random seed used in the Week-5 split and models is 42. The validation audit explicitly compares random and grouped splits and documents the leakage concern.
